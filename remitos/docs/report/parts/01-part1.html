1What borderless money is

Start from the physical reality: when money crosses a border, nothing physically crosses anything. Understanding that one fact explains most of what follows.

1.1 A definition

Borderless money is the ability to move value between countries as easily, cheaply and quickly as moving it within one — without the sender or recipient needing to understand, or pay for, the machinery in between.

That is an aspiration, not a description of the present. Today, sending Tk 5,000 from Dhaka to Chittagong is instant and nearly free. Sending the same value from Riyadh to Kishoreganj costs several percent and takes hours to days. The difference is not distance — it is that the second transaction crosses a monetary border, and monetary borders are where cost accumulates.

Borderless money is not the same as a cross-border payment

The distinction matters and is frequently blurred. A cross-border payment is a single transaction: value moves from A in one country to B in another. Cross-border payments have existed for centuries.

Borderless money is a property of a system: that the border stops being a meaningful obstacle. A system can process billions in cross-border payments and be nowhere near borderless, which is precisely the situation today.

The test Money is borderless when the user does not have to think about the border. By that test, no major corridor into Bangladesh is borderless today, and the report's job is to explain exactly why and what would change it.

1.2 Why the money does not actually travel

This is the single most useful thing for a newcomer to understand, and it is counter-intuitive.

When Abul in Riyadh sends SAR 1,000 to his mother, those riyals do not go anywhere. They stay in Saudi Arabia. What happens instead is that two institutions adjust their records of what they owe each other, and a corresponding amount of taka — which was already in Bangladesh — is released to his mother.

Cross-border payment is therefore not transport. It is bookkeeping between institutions that hold accounts with one another, plus a settlement process to true up the resulting imbalances.

Three consequences follow immediately, and they explain nearly every cost in the system:

ConsequenceWhy it costs money
Somebody must already hold taka in Bangladesh The payout institution needs local currency on hand before your transfer arrives. That money is pre-funded — parked, idle, and earning nothing. The cost of that idle capital is built into the fee you pay.
Somebody must bear exchange-rate risk Between quoting you a rate and settling the position, the rate moves. That risk is priced into the spread, which is usually a larger cost than the visible fee.
Somebody must prove the money is legitimate Anti-money-laundering obligations apply at every hop. The labour of proving compliance is a real, recurring cost — and it is largely manual, which is why it has barely fallen in twenty years.

Note what is not on that list: the technical cost of sending a message between two computers, which is effectively zero. Remittance is expensive because of capital, risk and compliance — not because of technology. Any project claiming to collapse remittance cost through a faster network has misdiagnosed the problem.

1.3 The four models of moving money across a border

Model 1 · Correspondent banking

The oldest arrangement. Bank A in Saudi Arabia holds an account with Bank B in Bangladesh, or both hold accounts with a third bank that bridges them. Instructions travel over the SWIFT messaging network; the accounts are adjusted; settlement follows.

These accounts have names worth knowing because they appear everywhere in the literature: from Bank A's perspective, its account held at Bank B is a Nostro account ("our money, held by you"); from Bank B's perspective, the same account is a Vostro ("your money, held by us"). The pre-funded taka sitting in that Nostro account is the idle capital described above.

Strengths: universal reach, regulated, handles large values. Weaknesses: slow, opaque, expensive, and it has been shrinking — banks have been withdrawing from correspondent relationships in developing markets because the compliance cost exceeds the revenue.

Model 2 · Money transfer operators (MTOs)

Western Union, MoneyGram, and the exchange houses in every Gulf labour district. The MTO maintains its own liquidity on both sides and its own payout network. A sender hands over cash; the recipient collects cash, often within minutes.

Strengths: fast, cash-to-cash, works without bank accounts on either side, enormous physical reach. Weaknesses: historically the most expensive channel, and the fee is often the smaller half of the cost — the exchange-rate margin is the rest.

Model 3 · Mobile-money interoperability

The model that has actually changed outcomes in South Asia and East Africa. Value moves into a mobile wallet — bKash, Nagad, Rocket — rather than a bank account. The recipient needs a phone, not a bank relationship.

Bangladesh's Binimoy platform, launched in November 2022, is the national layer that lets banks, MFS providers and payment service providers move money between each other rather than each operating a closed garden.[17][18]

Strengths: reaches the unbanked, cheap domestically, near-instant. Weaknesses: the wallet is capped by regulation, cash-out still depends on a physical agent, and interoperability remains under-used.

Model 4 · New rails — instant payment linkage, stablecoins, CBDCs

The frontier. Rather than routing through correspondent banks, connect countries' domestic instant-payment systems directly.

The reference implementation is the Bank for International Settlements' Project Nexus, which defines a scheme for linking domestic instant-payment systems through common APIs and ISO 20022 messages, targeting settlement in 60 seconds or less.[8][9] Nexus Global Payments was incorporated in 2025 by the central banks of India, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand, with a live target of 2027.[10][11]

A fact worth sitting with Bangladesh is not among the founding members of Nexus Global Payments. The five founders are India, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand.[10] Malaysia and Singapore are both significant sending corridors for Bangladeshi workers. A scheme that will make 60-second transfers routine across that region is being built, and Bangladesh is currently outside it. This is simultaneously the strongest argument for urgency and a constraint the architecture must design around rather than assume away.

1.4 Where remittance intelligence sits

All four models answer the question "how does value move?" None of them answers "which way should this particular transfer go?"

That second question is what the Congress Participants Guide names for this challenge area: compliance automation and routing optimisation, with a winning signal of lower cost, faster settlement. It is a genuinely different kind of problem — it is a prediction and optimisation problem, not a payments-infrastructure problem, and it is the one this report addresses.

2How cross-border money actually moves

Follow one transfer end to end. Every cost, delay and failure point in the rest of this report appears somewhere in this sequence.

2.1 The eight stages

Stage 1 · Initiation

The sender states an amount, a recipient and a payout preference. Nothing has moved. The critical thing that happens here is a quote: a promise about the exchange rate and the fee. The moment a provider quotes a binding rate, it has taken on exchange-rate risk, which is why quotes expire.

Stage 2 · Know Your Customer (KYC) and screening

The provider must establish who the sender is and that the transfer is lawful. In practice: identity document capture, verification, and screening both parties against sanctions lists published by the US Treasury's OFAC, the United Nations, the European Union and the UK.

This stage is where most of the per-transaction compliance cost lives, and where most of the friction that drives users to informal channels is created. It is also almost entirely manual at most providers.

Stage 3 · Funding

The sender actually pays — cash at a counter, card, local bank debit. The provider now holds value in the sending country.

Stage 4 · Foreign exchange

The provider converts at a rate that includes a margin over the wholesale rate. This margin is frequently larger than the advertised fee and is the reason the World Bank measures total cost rather than fees.

Why "total cost" is the only honest number A provider advertising "zero fees" may still be the most expensive option in a corridor, because the entire charge is embedded in the exchange rate. This is why RemitOS leads every screen with the landed amount — what actually arrives — rather than the fee. The fee is a component; the landed amount is the truth.

Stage 5 · Routing and correspondent movement

The instruction travels. Under Model 1 this is a SWIFT message through one or more correspondent banks, each of which may take a fee and adds a hop where the transfer can stall. Modern messaging uses the ISO 20022 standard, which carries structured, machine-readable data — including, importantly, the compliance data required by the FATF Travel Rule.

Stage 6 · Payout

The recipient's institution releases local currency: into a bank account, into an MFS wallet, or as cash at an agent point.

This is where Bangladesh's regulatory ceilings bite, and it is the stage most systems model least well. Bangladesh Bank caps agent cash-out at Tk 30,000 per day and Tk 200,000 per month, and caps a wallet balance at Tk 500,000.[15][16] A transfer that was perfectly valid at stages 1 through 5 can fail here, in front of the recipient, at a counter.

Stage 7 · Settlement

Behind the scenes, the institutions true up. This may happen hours or days after the recipient has their money — which is why the recipient's experience and the institution's settlement are two different timelines, and why pre-funding exists.

Stage 8 · Reconciliation and reporting

Both sides check their records against each other, investigate any discrepancy ("breaks"), and file the required regulatory reports. Unglamorous, and the stage where errors are actually caught.

2.3 Two vocabulary items worth knowing

TermWhat it means and why it matters here
ISO 20022 The international standard for financial messaging. Unlike older formats, it carries rich structured data — which is what makes automated compliance screening and automated reconciliation possible at all. It is also the message format Project Nexus uses.[8]
FATF Travel Rule A requirement from the Financial Action Task Force that identifying information about both the sender and the recipient must "travel" with a transfer above a threshold. It is the reason some data must cross borders even under a strict data-residency policy — a point handled explicitly in Chapter 16.
Where the AI opportunity actually is Read the eight stages again as a cost structure. Stage 2 (compliance) is manual labour. Stage 5 (routing) is an unoptimised choice. Stage 6 (payout) fails because constraints are checked too late. Stage 7 (settlement) is expensive because pre-funding is guesswork.

Every one of those four is a prediction or optimisation problem. That is not a coincidence, and it is the entire basis of this project.
