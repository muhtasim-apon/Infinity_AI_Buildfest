18Business model and unit economics

How the company makes money, what it refuses to do to make money, and the unit-economics model — presented as a model, because the numbers have not been measured.

18.1 Revenue lines

LineMechanismDependency
Transaction take-rateBasis points on landed value, set below the incumbent effective costVolume; partner rail cost
FX spread shareShared with the settlement partner, disclosed to the userPartner agreement
Compliance-as-a-serviceLicensing the screening and decisioning stack to smaller PSPs and banksProduct maturity; this is also what we trade for partner-of-record status
Agent SaaSFloat forecasting and reconciliation tooling for agent networksAgent adoption
Data productsAggregated, anonymised corridor benchmarks onlyVolume
An absolute constraint on the last line Selling anything derived from identifiable user behaviour is prohibited, not merely discouraged. The users of this product are people sending subsistence income across a border; their transaction patterns are not an asset to be monetised. Only corridor-level aggregates leave the building.

The third line is strategically more important than its likely revenue. Compliance-as-a-service is what a licensed partner actually wants from us, and it is therefore the currency in which we buy the licence, the float and the payout reach we do not have (Chapter 20).

18.2 The unit-economics model

Contribution per transfer
   =  (take_rate × landed_value)  +  fx_spread_share
   −  partner_rail_cost
   −  payout_cost
   −  variable_compliance_cost        ← human review minutes × loaded cost
   −  variable_infrastructure_cost    ← LLM tokens + in-boundary GPU

Break-even monthly volume  =  fixed_cost ÷ contribution_per_transfer

variable_compliance_cost is the term most fintech models omit, and it is the one our own architecture makes unavoidable. ADR 0010 mandates a human gate on every irreversible action, which means a measurable cost in officer-minutes per escalated transfer. Modelling it honestly is what makes the rest of the model credible — and it is also why the false-positive rate is a commercial metric as well as an ethical one: every unnecessary hold is both a harmed user and a paid review.

Why no numbers appear here A populated unit-economics table would be easy to produce and would look authoritative. It would also be a fabricated result presented as analysis, which §20.4 prohibits.

The team fills this model with measured figures during Phase 3 validation — partner quotes, observed review times, actual inference costs. Until then the structure is the deliverable and the numbers are blank, deliberately.

19.2 What actually breaks, and where

Technical scaling is the easy part, and saying so is more useful than a capacity table.

LimitMechanismDifficulty
Quote throughputRouting engine scales horizontally; predictions cached by corridor-amount bandEasy
Ledger write throughputGo service, partitioned by account; event-sourced appendModerate
RAG query volumeOff the synchronous path; in-boundary GPU is the cost driver, not the bottleneckModerate — cost, not capacity

Partner integrationEach rail is a bespoke contract and a bespoke integrationHard — commercial, not technical

The honest scaling statement This system scales technically to millions of transfers. It scales operationally only as fast as review capacity and partner integrations allow.

The lever that most improves scalability is therefore not infrastructure — it is reducing the false-positive rate, because every avoided unnecessary escalation is simultaneously a user not harmed and a reviewer-hour freed. Fairness and scalability point in the same direction here, which is unusual and worth exploiting.

19.3 Cost curve

Costs fall into three shapes, and knowing which is which is what makes the model predictable:

  • Fixed — the platform, the corpus, the evaluation suite. Amortised across every transfer, so the curve improves with volume.
  • Linear — partner rail costs, payout costs, human review. These do not improve with scale, which is why they must be modelled per transfer rather than assumed away.
  • Step — in-boundary GPU capacity, review team headcount, a new corridor integration. Each step is a discrete decision with a lead time attached.

20Go-to-market and the regulatory path

The chapter most student fintech projects skip, and the one that determines whether the product can legally exist.

20.1 What we are, legally

RemitOS as designed does not hold customer funds, operate as a money transfer operator, issue accounts or wallets, or set exchange rates. It orchestrates across licensed parties.

That is a deliberate regulatory posture, not a limitation discovered late: the orchestration layer is both the defensible product and the only position a new entrant can occupy without years of licensing first.

We are, however, not licence-free. Processing payment instructions and personal data on behalf of licensed institutions carries its own obligations, which is why the compliance, audit and ledger services are v1 scope rather than v2 ambitions.

21.2 What we deliberately do not measure

Excluded metricWhy
Registered usersBangladesh already has more registered MFS accounts than people.[13] Registration is a vanity number in this market and optimising it would actively mislead the team

21.3 Measurement discipline

Three commitments that make the numbers above trustworthy rather than decorative:

  • Every metric has an owner and appears in the daily operational digest (n8n workflow 10).
  • Fairness metrics appear per group in that digest, never aggregated — an aggregate hides the disparity the metric exists to surface.
  • Realised-versus-predicted is shown to users, not only internally. A product that publishes its own estimation error finds out quickly when a model is wrong.

23.3 The NRB collaboration (§8)

The Congress's NRB framework embeds a Non-Resident Bangladeshi or international expert as an active participant with a defined role. For this project the highest-value contribution is specific rather than general:

  • Corridor reality. Someone who has actually sent money from a Gulf state or the UK can correct assumptions about the sending experience that no amount of desk research reaches.
  • Regulatory perspective from the sending side. A corridor has two regulators (§20.2), and the sending-side one is the half a Dhaka-based team sees least.
  • Production engineering standards. Review of the architecture against how payment systems are actually operated at scale.
  • Introductions. Partner conversations are relationship-driven, and risk 1 in the register is precisely a partnership risk.

Per §8 and §20.8, all NRB contributions are transparently documented in the team documentation, and advisors may not influence judging.

25Domain-pack extensibility

Why this architecture outlives one idea — and how any FinSphere vertical drops into the same core.

25.1 The split

Roughly 70 % of what any FinSphere product needs is identical: identity, KYC, a ledger, risk scoring, a knowledge layer, agents, automation, observability, audit. The core knows nothing about remittance. Each vertical is a swappable domain pack supplying exactly four things and nothing else:

ElementContents
ConfigFeature flags, limits, rails, fee schedules, workflow bindings
SchemasDomain entities and events, versioned
PromptsSystem prompts, tool descriptions, retrieval templates
ModelsDomain-specific ML models and their cards

The core exposes stable extension points and never imports from a pack. The governing rule: a core change made to accommodate a vertical requires a new ADR — and if that happens twice for the same reason, the abstraction is wrong and gets redrawn.

25.2 The five FinSphere packs

PackChallenge areaWhat it reuses unchangedStatus
borderless_moneyCross-border payments and remittance intelligence—Active
creditCreditForAll — alternative-data credit scoringIdentity, KYC, ledger, explainability, fairness gates, audit. Plus: a verified remittance history is exactly the alternative data a thin-file borrower lacksStub
fraudFraudShield — real-time transaction defenceGraph, scoring pipeline, case management, human gates — already built here as a componentStub
wealthWealthPilot — advisory for everyday saversRAG with citation enforcement (suitability rules), personalisation, explainabilityStub
smeSME Capital Engine — working-capital intelligenceLedger, forecasting, document intelligence, compliance decisioningStub

The credit row is the strategically interesting one. A two-year verified remittance history is a genuine credit signal for a household with no formal financial record — which means the adjacent vertical is not a pivot but a compounding use of the same data, created as a by-product of the first product working.

25.3 Beyond FinSphere

Nothing in the core is finance-specific. The pattern — a domain-agnostic platform with identity, a knowledge layer, bounded agents, human gates, automation and audit — transfers to any regulated domain the Congress names. A HealthSphere pack would swap the corpus for clinical guidelines and the models for triage; the human gates, the residency boundary, the citation enforcement and the fairness machinery are identical because the underlying requirement is identical: an AI system making consequential decisions about people who need to be able to challenge them.

Why this was built this way A remittance orchestrator that cannot extend into credit or fraud is a feature, not a company. Building the abstraction from a real product — rather than speculatively ahead of one — is what makes it likely to be right. The four stub packs declare their extension points; they do not implement features nobody has validated.
