3.2 The cost of sending money — World Bank Remittance Prices Worldwide

The World Bank's Remittance Prices Worldwide (RPW) database is the authoritative international measurement of what it costs to send money. It covers 377 country corridors, from 48 sending countries to 111 receiving countries.[4] Its headline metric is the total cost — fee plus exchange-rate margin — of sending USD 200.

Three observations matter more than the headline.

First, progress has stalled. The global average moved from 6.26 % to 6.49 % and back to 6.36 % across three quarters — noise around a plateau, not a trend toward 3 %. Whatever is holding the cost up is structural, not a matter of waiting.

Second, the digital gap is the whole thesis. Digital costs 4.59 %; non-digital costs 7.30 %. That is a 2.71 percentage point difference for the same economic act. The technology to capture it already exists and is already measured.

Third, even digital is not good enough. At 4.59 %, fully digital remittance still sits more than 1.5 points above the SDG target. Digitising the channel is necessary and insufficient — which is precisely the argument for an intelligence layer on top rather than another digital wallet.

4Advantages of borderless money

What is actually gained when the border stops mattering — stated at the level of a household, not a white paper.

4.1 Cost compression, and what it means in practice

The abstract version: reducing total cost from 6.36 % toward 3 % roughly halves the extraction from every transfer.

The concrete version, which is the one that matters: a worker sending Tk 45,000 monthly at a 6.4 % total cost gives up roughly Tk 2,880 per transfer. At 3 %, that is about Tk 1,350. The difference, roughly Tk 1,530 a month, is around Tk 18,000 a year — a meaningful fraction of an annual school-fee burden for a rural household.

The arithmetic above is a straightforward illustration of published cost percentages applied to a stated amount. It is not a claim about what this product will achieve. No savings figure is claimed anywhere in this report.

4.2 Speed, and why it is not a convenience feature

In the developed-market framing, faster transfers are a nicety. In the remittance context they are frequently the difference between receiving treatment and not.

A significant share of remittance is event-driven: a medical emergency, a school deadline, a failed harvest, a funeral. When a transfer takes three days, the recipient either waits or borrows locally at rates that can exceed anything the transfer itself cost. Slow transfers push households into informal credit, and that second-order cost never appears in any remittance-cost index.

This is also the single largest reason hundi retains share. Hundi is often same-day. Competing on compliance messaging against a channel that delivers in two hours is not a strategy.

4.3 Financial inclusion — the honest version

Mobile money genuinely reaches people bank branches never did. Bangladesh now has more registered MFS accounts than it has people.[13]

But registration is not inclusion. bKash reports roughly 42 million active users against a 39.9 % market share, and Nagad roughly 28.2 million.[13] The gap between "registered accounts exceed the population" and "70 million active users" is the actual inclusion story, and it is a story about usage, not sign-up.

A metric discipline adopted from this finding Because registered accounts already exceed the population, registration is a vanity metric in this market. Chapter 21 therefore excludes it from the KPI set entirely and measures active monthly senders, rural recipient share, and IVR/USSD flow completion instead.

4.4 Exchange-rate transparency

Because the exchange margin is usually larger than the visible fee, opacity here is where most value is quietly extracted. A borderless system that displays the landed amount — what actually arrives — converts a hidden cost into a comparable one. Comparability is what creates competitive pressure, and competitive pressure is what actually moves prices.

4.5 A compliance trail that works for the honest user

This advantage is usually framed from the regulator's perspective. Framed from the user's, it is more interesting: a formal transfer creates a verifiable record of income. That record supports credit access, property transactions and visa applications. Hundi creates nothing.

The record is therefore not merely a regulatory obligation — it is an asset the formal channel can offer and the informal channel structurally cannot. It is also the bridge into the adjacent domain packs described in Chapter 25: a verified two-year remittance history is precisely the alternative data an unbanked borrower lacks.

4.6 Diaspora capital formation

Remittance is currently almost entirely consumption transfer. A borderless system with a programmable layer can, without changing that default, make it possible for a diaspora saver to direct a portion into a local deposit, a bond, or a family business — converting a consumption flow into a partial investment flow. This is a long-horizon opportunity rather than a v1 feature, and it is noted here rather than claimed.

4.7 Macroeconomic advantage

Formal inflows enter reserves; informal inflows do not. The academic estimate that the disparity between actual and potential reserves attributable to hundi is around 19.2 % gives the scale of what formalisation is worth.[20] For a country managing external balances, shifting flow from informal to formal is a monetary-policy outcome, not merely a fintech one.

The advantage that subsumes the rest Every advantage above reduces to one thing: choice under information. Today a sender chooses between channels without knowing what any of them will actually deliver. Given the landed amount and the arrival time for every option, the market becomes competitive — and competitive markets do the cost compression by themselves. The product's job is to supply the information.
