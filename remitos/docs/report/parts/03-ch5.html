5Limitations in Bangladesh — the diagnosis

Eleven constraints, in rough order of how much money they move. Each is stated with its mechanism, not merely named — because Chapter 6 addresses each one individually, and a remedy can only be as good as the diagnosis behind it.

5.1 Hundi — the informal channel, and why it keeps winning

Hundi is an informal value-transfer system that predates modern banking. Its mechanism is elegant and requires no money to cross a border at all: a worker in Riyadh hands cash to an operator there; the operator instructs a counterpart in Bangladesh to pay out the equivalent in taka from funds already held locally; the two operators settle between themselves later, often by netting against flows in the opposite direction, or through trade invoicing.

The scale is contested, which is expected for an unrecorded activity, and the report states the range rather than picking a convenient number:

The mechanism of hundi's advantage is what matters, not its size. It wins on four dimensions simultaneously:

DimensionWhy hundi wins
RateOperating outside the official exchange-rate mechanism, hundi can offer a rate closer to the kerb market — sometimes materially better than the official rate available through formal channels.
SpeedFrequently same-day, sometimes within hours. No correspondent hops, no settlement wait, because no money crosses a border.
DeliveryCash to the door in a village. No travel to an agent point, no queue, no counter.
PaperworkNone. No identity document, no source-of-funds question, no verification delay.
The strategic conclusion most projects get wrong Hundi is not chosen out of ignorance, and it is not defeated by explaining that it is illegal. The migrant worker using it usually knows. He uses it because it delivers more taka, faster, to his mother's door, with no forms.

Any product that does not compete on those four dimensions will not convert a single hundi user, however sophisticated its compliance story. This single finding shapes the entire product design that follows.

5.2 Foreign-exchange constraints and the rate gap

Bangladesh operates a managed exchange-rate regime. Two consequences follow.

First, "the rate" is not one number. There is an official rate through formal channels and an effective kerb-market rate available informally. When a gap opens between them, it is a direct subsidy to hundi — the informal channel can simply pass part of the difference to the sender and still profit.

Second, periods of foreign-currency scarcity create pressure on payout institutions to ration liquidity, which shows up to users as delays and to the market as a wider effective spread.

The government's 2.5 % remittance incentive exists to close this gap by paying formal senders a premium. It works, and it is also a market distortion with two side effects: it makes channel economics depend on a policy that could change, and the eligibility rules are not well understood by the senders they are meant to attract.

5.3 The outbound restriction — structural, not technical

Bangladesh restricts outbound current and capital-account transactions. The country is, in practice, a receive-only node in the global money graph.

The clearest evidence is PayPal. Bangladeshi residents cannot open a standard PayPal account, hold a PayPal balance, or send payments with Bangladesh as their registered country — because PayPal's two-way model is incompatible with the outbound restriction.[25][26] Xoom, PayPal's remittance subsidiary, operates inbound only and is limited to non-commercial transfers; commercial use can trigger account termination.[27][28] A Payoneer–PayPal integration launched in late 2025 gives eligible freelancers a partial workaround.[25]

Why the PayPal absence belongs in this report It is the cleanest available proof that Bangladesh's problem is regulatory-architectural, not technological. PayPal did not fail to build a Bangladesh product. The country's monetary architecture does not admit one.

Any honest "borderless money" thesis for Bangladesh must state this and design within it. Chapter 10 accordingly lists outbound payments as explicitly out of scope.

5.4 The last-mile agent bottleneck

MFS agent networks are the reason mobile money reached rural Bangladesh at all. They are also the point where the user experience is thinnest.

A recipient in a rural upazila may travel 30–60 minutes to reach an agent point. On arrival, four things can go wrong that have nothing to do with the transfer itself: the agent's float has run out; the amount exceeds a daily ceiling; the agent charges above the posted rate; or the agent is simply closed. Each failure costs the recipient another journey, and a formal channel that fails twice has taught a household to use hundi.

These limits are reasonable financial-crime controls. The problem is not the limits — it is that no consumer product treats them as part of the transaction design.

Consider the routine case. A worker sends Tk 120,000 for a medical emergency. Every conventional product quotes a price, accepts payment, and pays out to a wallet. The recipient — often an older woman who has travelled 40 minutes — can withdraw Tk 30,000. The remaining Tk 90,000 sits in a wallet she may not know how to move, during an emergency.

The money was never lost. The product simply never modelled the constraint. This single observation drives the central design decision of RemitOS and is treated at length in §6.5 and §9.2.

Two readings of the same data. The optimistic one: smartphone adoption is rising fast. The one that matters here: more than a quarter of users have no smartphone, and roughly 69 million mobile connections carry no data at all.

These are not edge cases. They are disproportionately rural, older and lower-income — which is to say, disproportionately the people who depend most on remittance income. A product reachable only by a modern smartphone on a data plan excludes its most dependent users by construction.

5.7 Literacy, language and financial vocabulary

Three distinct barriers that are frequently collapsed into one:

  • Text literacy. A recipient who cannot reliably read a transaction SMS asks a neighbour — which means her balance is not private, and she is dependent on someone else's honesty about her own money.
  • Digital literacy. Navigating a multi-screen flow is a learned skill. Someone who has never used an app does not fail at remittance; they fail at menus.
  • Financial vocabulary. Terms like "exchange rate", "settlement" and "beneficiary" have no comfortable everyday Bangla equivalent, and literal translation produces English-in-Bengali-script that native speakers do not actually use.

To which a fourth, usually invisible, problem must be added: numeral and grouping conventions. Bangladesh groups digits as 1,20,000 (lakh convention), not 120,000. Many older users read Bengali numerals (১২৩) fluently and Arabic numerals slowly. A money screen that gets either wrong signals, instantly and correctly, that the product was not built for this country.

5.8 Interoperability gaps

Binimoy, launched November 2022, is the national platform allowing banks, MFS operators and PSPs to move money between one another.[17][18] Published costs are Tk 5 per thousand for MFS-to-MFS and MFS-to-PSP, and Tk 10 per thousand for MFS-to-bank.[19]

The infrastructure exists and is under-used. The practical effect is that a recipient's money frequently lands in the wrong container: in a wallet when they need it in a bank account, or at an agent point when a bank transfer would have cleared the ceiling problem. The rails to move between them exist; almost nothing chooses between them on the user's behalf.

5.9 Identity verification friction

KYC is required and is the largest single source of onboarding abandonment. The specific Bangladeshi difficulties compound each other:

  • National ID cards are frequently old, laminated, worn or physically damaged — the normal case, not the edge case.
  • Bengali-script document faces are harder to read automatically than Latin ones.
  • Name transliteration is non-deterministic: রহিমা legitimately romanises as Rahima, Rohima or Rahema. Systems doing exact string matching on names therefore both miss real sanctions hits and generate false positives against ordinary people with common names.
  • Address verification is difficult where formal addressing is thin.

5.10 Trust deficit and fraud exposure

Social-engineering fraud is widespread and specifically targets the remittance relationship: a caller claims a relative is in hospital and money must be sent now. Urgency is the attack vector, and urgency is also the genuine condition of many real transfers — which makes the two hard to separate.

The consequence is structural rather than incidental. Once defrauded, a household's response is rarely to switch providers. It is to revert to the channel it already trusts — a known local hundi operator, who is a person rather than an interface.

5.11 The incentive as a distortion

The 2.5 % government remittance incentive is effective at pulling flow into formal channels. It also has three side effects worth stating: channel economics become dependent on a policy decision that could be withdrawn; eligibility rules are poorly understood by the senders they target; and it masks the underlying cost problem rather than solving it, because a channel that is uncompetitive at 6.4 % remains uncompetitive — it is simply being subsidised.
