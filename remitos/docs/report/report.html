§Abstract

Bangladesh does not have a remittance volume problem. It has a routing, compliance and trust problem — and those are solvable with the tools that exist today.

In the fiscal year ending June 2026, Bangladesh received USD 35.56 billion in formal remittances, the largest annual inflow in its history and the seventh largest in the world.[5][6] Over the same period, the World Bank recorded a global average cost of 6.36 % to send USD 200, against a United Nations Sustainable Development Goal target of 3 %.[1][3] Academic estimates place roughly 49 % of the money flowing toward Bangladesh outside the formal system entirely, moving through the informal hundi network.[20]

These three numbers describe a market that is enormous, expensive, and half invisible. They do not, however, describe a technology gap. Bangladesh has fourteen licensed mobile financial services providers moving roughly Tk 6,000 crore every day, a national interoperability platform, and more registered MFS accounts than it has people.[12][13] The rails exist.

What does not exist is a neutral party that computes the routing decision. When a construction worker in Riyadh sends money to his mother in Kishoreganj, no system tells him which combination of corridor, rail, exchange-rate window and payout endpoint will place the most taka in her hands, soonest, within the law. He guesses. Roughly half the time, he guesses hundi — because it is faster, the rate is often better, and nobody asks him for paperwork.

This report specifies RemitOS: an AI-native intelligence and orchestration layer that sits above every existing rail rather than competing with any of them. It has three load-bearing capabilities. A machine-learned routing engine that predicts landed cost and arrival time for every feasible path and solves under regulatory constraints. A compliance engine grounded in retrieval over Bangladesh Bank circulars and FATF guidance, which cites the rule behind every verdict or refuses to answer. And a knowledge graph that surfaces the network signatures per-transaction scoring cannot see.

The report is written for a reader with no prior knowledge of cross-border payments. It defines borderless money from first principles, traces how money physically moves between countries, quantifies the opportunity from international sources, diagnoses why Bangladesh specifically underperforms, and then specifies a production architecture — Flutter across Android, iOS and Web; fourteen microservices; eight machine-learning models; retrieval-augmented generation with citation enforcement; five bounded AI agents; and twelve automation workflows.

It also states, in a dedicated chapter, what this project cannot do. We have no payments licence, no settlement float, no agent network, and no supervisory relationship. Bangladesh's restriction on outbound transactions — the same restriction that keeps PayPal unavailable in the country — is a capital-account policy question that no application can solve. Naming those limits is not a weakness in the submission. A project claiming to have solved cross-border payments without addressing licensing has not built a business; it has built a demo.

The thesis in one sentence Every existing player is a rail. Rails are not the gap. The gap is the decision — and the decision is an AI problem.

1What borderless money is

Start from the physical reality: when money crosses a border, nothing physically crosses anything. Understanding that one fact explains most of what follows.

1.1 A definition

Borderless money is the ability to move value between countries as easily, cheaply and quickly as moving it within one — without the sender or recipient needing to understand, or pay for, the machinery in between.

That is an aspiration, not a description of the present. Today, sending Tk 5,000 from Dhaka to Chittagong is instant and nearly free. Sending the same value from Riyadh to Kishoreganj costs several percent and takes hours to days. The difference is not distance — it is that the second transaction crosses a monetary border, and monetary borders are where cost accumulates.

Borderless money is not the same as a cross-border payment

The distinction matters and is frequently blurred. A cross-border payment is a single transaction: value moves from A in one country to B in another. Cross-border payments have existed for centuries.

Borderless money is a property of a system: that the border stops being a meaningful obstacle. A system can process billions in cross-border payments and be nowhere near borderless, which is precisely the situation today.

The test Money is borderless when the user does not have to think about the border. By that test, no major corridor into Bangladesh is borderless today, and the report's job is to explain exactly why and what would change it.

1.2 Why the money does not actually travel

This is the single most useful thing for a newcomer to understand, and it is counter-intuitive.

When Abul in Riyadh sends SAR 1,000 to his mother, those riyals do not go anywhere. They stay in Saudi Arabia. What happens instead is that two institutions adjust their records of what they owe each other, and a corresponding amount of taka — which was already in Bangladesh — is released to his mother.

Cross-border payment is therefore not transport. It is bookkeeping between institutions that hold accounts with one another, plus a settlement process to true up the resulting imbalances.

Three consequences follow immediately, and they explain nearly every cost in the system:

ConsequenceWhy it costs money
Somebody must already hold taka in Bangladesh The payout institution needs local currency on hand before your transfer arrives. That money is pre-funded — parked, idle, and earning nothing. The cost of that idle capital is built into the fee you pay.
Somebody must bear exchange-rate risk Between quoting you a rate and settling the position, the rate moves. That risk is priced into the spread, which is usually a larger cost than the visible fee.
Somebody must prove the money is legitimate Anti-money-laundering obligations apply at every hop. The labour of proving compliance is a real, recurring cost — and it is largely manual, which is why it has barely fallen in twenty years.

Note what is not on that list: the technical cost of sending a message between two computers, which is effectively zero. Remittance is expensive because of capital, risk and compliance — not because of technology. Any project claiming to collapse remittance cost through a faster network has misdiagnosed the problem.

1.3 The four models of moving money across a border

Model 1 · Correspondent banking

The oldest arrangement. Bank A in Saudi Arabia holds an account with Bank B in Bangladesh, or both hold accounts with a third bank that bridges them. Instructions travel over the SWIFT messaging network; the accounts are adjusted; settlement follows.

These accounts have names worth knowing because they appear everywhere in the literature: from Bank A's perspective, its account held at Bank B is a Nostro account ("our money, held by you"); from Bank B's perspective, the same account is a Vostro ("your money, held by us"). The pre-funded taka sitting in that Nostro account is the idle capital described above.

Strengths: universal reach, regulated, handles large values. Weaknesses: slow, opaque, expensive, and it has been shrinking — banks have been withdrawing from correspondent relationships in developing markets because the compliance cost exceeds the revenue.

Model 2 · Money transfer operators (MTOs)

Western Union, MoneyGram, and the exchange houses in every Gulf labour district. The MTO maintains its own liquidity on both sides and its own payout network. A sender hands over cash; the recipient collects cash, often within minutes.

Strengths: fast, cash-to-cash, works without bank accounts on either side, enormous physical reach. Weaknesses: historically the most expensive channel, and the fee is often the smaller half of the cost — the exchange-rate margin is the rest.

Model 3 · Mobile-money interoperability

The model that has actually changed outcomes in South Asia and East Africa. Value moves into a mobile wallet — bKash, Nagad, Rocket — rather than a bank account. The recipient needs a phone, not a bank relationship.

Bangladesh's Binimoy platform, launched in November 2022, is the national layer that lets banks, MFS providers and payment service providers move money between each other rather than each operating a closed garden.[17][18]

Strengths: reaches the unbanked, cheap domestically, near-instant. Weaknesses: the wallet is capped by regulation, cash-out still depends on a physical agent, and interoperability remains under-used.

Model 4 · New rails — instant payment linkage, stablecoins, CBDCs

The frontier. Rather than routing through correspondent banks, connect countries' domestic instant-payment systems directly.

The reference implementation is the Bank for International Settlements' Project Nexus, which defines a scheme for linking domestic instant-payment systems through common APIs and ISO 20022 messages, targeting settlement in 60 seconds or less.[8][9] Nexus Global Payments was incorporated in 2025 by the central banks of India, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand, with a live target of 2027.[10][11]

A fact worth sitting with Bangladesh is not among the founding members of Nexus Global Payments. The five founders are India, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand.[10] Malaysia and Singapore are both significant sending corridors for Bangladeshi workers. A scheme that will make 60-second transfers routine across that region is being built, and Bangladesh is currently outside it. This is simultaneously the strongest argument for urgency and a constraint the architecture must design around rather than assume away.

1.4 Where remittance intelligence sits

All four models answer the question "how does value move?" None of them answers "which way should this particular transfer go?"

That second question is what the Congress Participants Guide names for this challenge area: compliance automation and routing optimisation, with a winning signal of lower cost, faster settlement. It is a genuinely different kind of problem — it is a prediction and optimisation problem, not a payments-infrastructure problem, and it is the one this report addresses.

2How cross-border money actually moves

Follow one transfer end to end. Every cost, delay and failure point in the rest of this report appears somewhere in this sequence.

2.1 The eight stages

Stage 1 · Initiation

The sender states an amount, a recipient and a payout preference. Nothing has moved. The critical thing that happens here is a quote: a promise about the exchange rate and the fee. The moment a provider quotes a binding rate, it has taken on exchange-rate risk, which is why quotes expire.

Stage 2 · Know Your Customer (KYC) and screening

The provider must establish who the sender is and that the transfer is lawful. In practice: identity document capture, verification, and screening both parties against sanctions lists published by the US Treasury's OFAC, the United Nations, the European Union and the UK.

This stage is where most of the per-transaction compliance cost lives, and where most of the friction that drives users to informal channels is created. It is also almost entirely manual at most providers.

Stage 3 · Funding

The sender actually pays — cash at a counter, card, local bank debit. The provider now holds value in the sending country.

Stage 4 · Foreign exchange

The provider converts at a rate that includes a margin over the wholesale rate. This margin is frequently larger than the advertised fee and is the reason the World Bank measures total cost rather than fees.

Why "total cost" is the only honest number A provider advertising "zero fees" may still be the most expensive option in a corridor, because the entire charge is embedded in the exchange rate. This is why RemitOS leads every screen with the landed amount — what actually arrives — rather than the fee. The fee is a component; the landed amount is the truth.

Stage 5 · Routing and correspondent movement

The instruction travels. Under Model 1 this is a SWIFT message through one or more correspondent banks, each of which may take a fee and adds a hop where the transfer can stall. Modern messaging uses the ISO 20022 standard, which carries structured, machine-readable data — including, importantly, the compliance data required by the FATF Travel Rule.

Stage 6 · Payout

The recipient's institution releases local currency: into a bank account, into an MFS wallet, or as cash at an agent point.

This is where Bangladesh's regulatory ceilings bite, and it is the stage most systems model least well. Bangladesh Bank caps agent cash-out at Tk 30,000 per day and Tk 200,000 per month, and caps a wallet balance at Tk 500,000.[15][16] A transfer that was perfectly valid at stages 1 through 5 can fail here, in front of the recipient, at a counter.

Stage 7 · Settlement

Behind the scenes, the institutions true up. This may happen hours or days after the recipient has their money — which is why the recipient's experience and the institution's settlement are two different timelines, and why pre-funding exists.

Stage 8 · Reconciliation and reporting

Both sides check their records against each other, investigate any discrepancy ("breaks"), and file the required regulatory reports. Unglamorous, and the stage where errors are actually caught.

4Advantages of borderless money

What is actually gained when the border stops mattering — stated at the level of a household, not a white paper.

4.1 Cost compression, and what it means in practice

The abstract version: reducing total cost from 6.36 % toward 3 % roughly halves the extraction from every transfer.

The concrete version, which is the one that matters: a worker sending Tk 45,000 monthly at a 6.4 % total cost gives up roughly Tk 2,880 per transfer. At 3 %, that is about Tk 1,350. The difference, roughly Tk 1,530 a month, is around Tk 18,000 a year — a meaningful fraction of an annual school-fee burden for a rural household.

The arithmetic above is a straightforward illustration of published cost percentages applied to a stated amount. It is not a claim about what this product will achieve. No savings figure is claimed anywhere in this report.

4.2 Speed, and why it is not a convenience feature

In the developed-market framing, faster transfers are a nicety. In the remittance context they are frequently the difference between receiving treatment and not.

A significant share of remittance is event-driven: a medical emergency, a school deadline, a failed harvest, a funeral. When a transfer takes three days, the recipient either waits or borrows locally at rates that can exceed anything the transfer itself cost. Slow transfers push households into informal credit, and that second-order cost never appears in any remittance-cost index.

This is also the single largest reason hundi retains share. Hundi is often same-day. Competing on compliance messaging against a channel that delivers in two hours is not a strategy.

4.3 Financial inclusion — the honest version

Mobile money genuinely reaches people bank branches never did. Bangladesh now has more registered MFS accounts than it has people.[13]

But registration is not inclusion. bKash reports roughly 42 million active users against a 39.9 % market share, and Nagad roughly 28.2 million.[13] The gap between "registered accounts exceed the population" and "70 million active users" is the actual inclusion story, and it is a story about usage, not sign-up.

A metric discipline adopted from this finding Because registered accounts already exceed the population, registration is a vanity metric in this market. Chapter 21 therefore excludes it from the KPI set entirely and measures active monthly senders, rural recipient share, and IVR/USSD flow completion instead.

4.4 Exchange-rate transparency

Because the exchange margin is usually larger than the visible fee, opacity here is where most value is quietly extracted. A borderless system that displays the landed amount — what actually arrives — converts a hidden cost into a comparable one. Comparability is what creates competitive pressure, and competitive pressure is what actually moves prices.

4.5 A compliance trail that works for the honest user

This advantage is usually framed from the regulator's perspective. Framed from the user's, it is more interesting: a formal transfer creates a verifiable record of income. That record supports credit access, property transactions and visa applications. Hundi creates nothing.

The record is therefore not merely a regulatory obligation — it is an asset the formal channel can offer and the informal channel structurally cannot. It is also the bridge into the adjacent domain packs described in Chapter 25: a verified two-year remittance history is precisely the alternative data an unbanked borrower lacks.

4.6 Diaspora capital formation

Remittance is currently almost entirely consumption transfer. A borderless system with a programmable layer can, without changing that default, make it possible for a diaspora saver to direct a portion into a local deposit, a bond, or a family business — converting a consumption flow into a partial investment flow. This is a long-horizon opportunity rather than a v1 feature, and it is noted here rather than claimed.

4.7 Macroeconomic advantage

Formal inflows enter reserves; informal inflows do not. The academic estimate that the disparity between actual and potential reserves attributable to hundi is around 19.2 % gives the scale of what formalisation is worth.[20] For a country managing external balances, shifting flow from informal to formal is a monetary-policy outcome, not merely a fintech one.

The advantage that subsumes the rest Every advantage above reduces to one thing: choice under information. Today a sender chooses between channels without knowing what any of them will actually deliver. Given the landed amount and the arrival time for every option, the market becomes competitive — and competitive markets do the cost compression by themselves. The product's job is to supply the information.

5Limitations in Bangladesh — the diagnosis

Eleven constraints, in rough order of how much money they move. Each is stated with its mechanism, not merely named — because Chapter 6 addresses each one individually, and a remedy can only be as good as the diagnosis behind it.

5.1 Hundi — the informal channel, and why it keeps winning

Hundi is an informal value-transfer system that predates modern banking. Its mechanism is elegant and requires no money to cross a border at all: a worker in Riyadh hands cash to an operator there; the operator instructs a counterpart in Bangladesh to pay out the equivalent in taka from funds already held locally; the two operators settle between themselves later, often by netting against flows in the opposite direction, or through trade invoicing.

The scale is contested, which is expected for an unrecorded activity, and the report states the range rather than picking a convenient number:

The mechanism of hundi's advantage is what matters, not its size. It wins on four dimensions simultaneously:

The strategic conclusion most projects get wrong Hundi is not chosen out of ignorance, and it is not defeated by explaining that it is illegal. The migrant worker using it usually knows. He uses it because it delivers more taka, faster, to his mother's door, with no forms.

Any product that does not compete on those four dimensions will not convert a single hundi user, however sophisticated its compliance story. This single finding shapes the entire product design that follows.

5.2 Foreign-exchange constraints and the rate gap

Bangladesh operates a managed exchange-rate regime. Two consequences follow.

First, "the rate" is not one number. There is an official rate through formal channels and an effective kerb-market rate available informally. When a gap opens between them, it is a direct subsidy to hundi — the informal channel can simply pass part of the difference to the sender and still profit.

Second, periods of foreign-currency scarcity create pressure on payout institutions to ration liquidity, which shows up to users as delays and to the market as a wider effective spread.

The government's 2.5 % remittance incentive exists to close this gap by paying formal senders a premium. It works, and it is also a market distortion with two side effects: it makes channel economics depend on a policy that could change, and the eligibility rules are not well understood by the senders they are meant to attract.

5.3 The outbound restriction — structural, not technical

Bangladesh restricts outbound current and capital-account transactions. The country is, in practice, a receive-only node in the global money graph.

The clearest evidence is PayPal. Bangladeshi residents cannot open a standard PayPal account, hold a PayPal balance, or send payments with Bangladesh as their registered country — because PayPal's two-way model is incompatible with the outbound restriction.[25][26] Xoom, PayPal's remittance subsidiary, operates inbound only and is limited to non-commercial transfers; commercial use can trigger account termination.[27][28] A Payoneer–PayPal integration launched in late 2025 gives eligible freelancers a partial workaround.[25]

Why the PayPal absence belongs in this report It is the cleanest available proof that Bangladesh's problem is regulatory-architectural, not technological. PayPal did not fail to build a Bangladesh product. The country's monetary architecture does not admit one.

Any honest "borderless money" thesis for Bangladesh must state this and design within it. Chapter 10 accordingly lists outbound payments as explicitly out of scope.

5.4 The last-mile agent bottleneck

MFS agent networks are the reason mobile money reached rural Bangladesh at all. They are also the point where the user experience is thinnest.

A recipient in a rural upazila may travel 30–60 minutes to reach an agent point. On arrival, four things can go wrong that have nothing to do with the transfer itself: the agent's float has run out; the amount exceeds a daily ceiling; the agent charges above the posted rate; or the agent is simply closed. Each failure costs the recipient another journey, and a formal channel that fails twice has taught a household to use hundi.

Consider the routine case. A worker sends Tk 120,000 for a medical emergency. Every conventional product quotes a price, accepts payment, and pays out to a wallet. The recipient — often an older woman who has travelled 40 minutes — can withdraw Tk 30,000. The remaining Tk 90,000 sits in a wallet she may not know how to move, during an emergency.

The money was never lost. The product simply never modelled the constraint. This single observation drives the central design decision of RemitOS and is treated at length in §6.5 and §9.2.

5.7 Literacy, language and financial vocabulary

Three distinct barriers that are frequently collapsed into one:

  • Text literacy. A recipient who cannot reliably read a transaction SMS asks a neighbour — which means her balance is not private, and she is dependent on someone else's honesty about her own money.
  • Digital literacy. Navigating a multi-screen flow is a learned skill. Someone who has never used an app does not fail at remittance; they fail at menus.
  • Financial vocabulary. Terms like "exchange rate", "settlement" and "beneficiary" have no comfortable everyday Bangla equivalent, and literal translation produces English-in-Bengali-script that native speakers do not actually use.

To which a fourth, usually invisible, problem must be added: numeral and grouping conventions. Bangladesh groups digits as 1,20,000 (lakh convention), not 120,000. Many older users read Bengali numerals (১২৩) fluently and Arabic numerals slowly. A money screen that gets either wrong signals, instantly and correctly, that the product was not built for this country.

5.8 Interoperability gaps

Binimoy, launched November 2022, is the national platform allowing banks, MFS operators and PSPs to move money between one another.[17][18] Published costs are Tk 5 per thousand for MFS-to-MFS and MFS-to-PSP, and Tk 10 per thousand for MFS-to-bank.[19]

The infrastructure exists and is under-used. The practical effect is that a recipient's money frequently lands in the wrong container: in a wallet when they need it in a bank account, or at an agent point when a bank transfer would have cleared the ceiling problem. The rails to move between them exist; almost nothing chooses between them on the user's behalf.

5.9 Identity verification friction

KYC is required and is the largest single source of onboarding abandonment. The specific Bangladeshi difficulties compound each other:

  • National ID cards are frequently old, laminated, worn or physically damaged — the normal case, not the edge case.
  • Bengali-script document faces are harder to read automatically than Latin ones.
  • Name transliteration is non-deterministic: রহিমা legitimately romanises as Rahima, Rohima or Rahema. Systems doing exact string matching on names therefore both miss real sanctions hits and generate false positives against ordinary people with common names.
  • Address verification is difficult where formal addressing is thin.

5.10 Trust deficit and fraud exposure

Social-engineering fraud is widespread and specifically targets the remittance relationship: a caller claims a relative is in hospital and money must be sent now. Urgency is the attack vector, and urgency is also the genuine condition of many real transfers — which makes the two hard to separate.

The consequence is structural rather than incidental. Once defrauded, a household's response is rarely to switch providers. It is to revert to the channel it already trusts — a known local hundi operator, who is a person rather than an interface.

5.11 The incentive as a distortion

The 2.5 % government remittance incentive is effective at pulling flow into formal channels. It also has three side effects worth stating: channel economics become dependent on a policy decision that could be withdrawn; eligibility rules are poorly understood by the senders they target; and it masks the underlying cost problem rather than solving it, because a channel that is uncompetitive at 6.4 % remains uncompetitive — it is simply being subsidised.

Roughly 69 million mobile connections carry no data. Two mechanisms, not one.

Offline-first in the app. The network is treated as an unreliable synchroniser rather than a prerequisite. 6.5 The transaction-ceiling remedy — the central design decision

This is the mechanism that most distinguishes RemitOS, and it is worth stating precisely.

Regulatory ceilings are inputs to the routing optimiser, not validations performed after the fact. Before any price is shown, the constraint filter computes the remaining legal capacity at each endpoint, given the recipient's usage today and this month and — for wallets — the balance ceiling. The engine then returns one of three honest outcomes:

  1. A single compliant path, where one endpoint can clear the full amount legally.
  2. A proposed legal split: for example Tk 30,000 to the wallet for same-day cash-out and Tk 90,000 to a linked bank account tomorrow, both legs priced, timed, and explained in Bangla on one screen.
  3. Explicit infeasibility, stated before the sender pays, with concrete alternatives.

A subtlety worth noting: headroom is a property of the recipient, not of the partner, and it is consumed across every sender paying that recipient. A route that looks available in isolation may not be — which is exactly the case a post-hoc validation misses.

6.6 Reducing KYC friction without weakening due diligence

The response to KYC-driven abandonment is not lighter due diligence. It is due diligence that is fast, assisted, and honest about its timeline.

  • OCR extraction trained on the documents people actually carry — worn, laminated, photographed on a low-end camera in poor light. A model trained on clean scans fails precisely for the users who most need it to work.
  • Transliteration-aware matching. Fuzzy and phonetic comparison, never exact string equality, so that a legitimately variant romanisation of a common Bangla name is neither a missed sanctions hit nor an unfair false positive.
  • Agent-assisted capture for users who cannot self-serve, with the agent's identity logged on every action — which protects honest agents as much as it deters dishonest ones.
  • A stated time commitment. A ten-minute clear answer beats a two-day silence, and silence is what returns a household to hundi.

7.1 At the scale of a household

Remittance in Bangladesh is not discretionary income. It is, for millions of households, the income — paying for school fees, medicine, roof repairs, and the debt taken on to fund the migration in the first place.

Two consequences follow that are invisible in aggregate statistics. First, cost is not an inconvenience but a direct reduction in a household's consumption. Second, delay is not an inconvenience either: a household waiting three days for emergency funds frequently borrows locally at rates that dwarf anything the transfer cost. That second-order cost appears in no remittance-price index anywhere.

The target will not be met by waiting for costs to fall. It will be met, if at all, by building the systems that make the digital channel — already 2.71 points cheaper — both universally reachable and genuinely optimised. That is a specific, buildable engineering agenda rather than an aspiration.

9How RemitOS overrides the existing process

"Override" does not mean replace. bKash will still be the wallet and the bank will still settle. What changes is who decides.

9.1 The positioning

The tempting pitch is "a better bKash". It is also the wrong one: distribution is saturated, the incumbent has fifteen years of agent build-out, and a new wallet competes on exactly the dimension where a new entrant is weakest.

The defensible position is the one nobody occupies: intelligence and orchestration above every rail, treating bKash, Nagad, Rocket, banks, agents and MTOs as interchangeable endpoints rather than as competitors.

9.2 Four specific overrides

Override 1 · The routing decision becomes computed rather than guessed

Today: the sender picks a provider, sees one price from one rail, and has no way to know whether another path would have delivered more. He compares three providers by hand, badly, every month.

With RemitOS: every feasible path is enumerated, its landed amount and arrival time predicted, the illegal ones removed, and the survivors ranked — with a one-line Bangla explanation of the trade-off. The user makes an informed choice in seconds, which is a decision he could not previously make at all.

Override 2 · Regulatory limits stop being a counter-side surprise

Today: quote, pay, pay out to wallet, discover the Tk 30,000 cash-out ceiling at the agent counter after a 40-minute journey.

With RemitOS: the ceiling is a constraint in the optimiser. The system proposes a legal split, prices both legs, and explains it in Bangla — before payment. See §6.5 and Figure 6.1.

This is the override that is most visible in thirty seconds on a stage, and the one that most clearly demonstrates a system that has read the regulation rather than only the API documentation.

Override 3 · Compliance becomes explainable and fast

Today: a held transfer produces silence, then a customer-service queue, then — sometimes — an explanation. Silence during a held emergency transfer is the single most effective driver of a household back to hundi.

With RemitOS: an immediate notification, a plain-Bangla reason, a stated SLA, and a visible appeal route to a human. Behind it, the decision is grounded in cited regulation — and if no governing rule can be retrieved with sufficient confidence, the system refuses to decide and escalates rather than guessing.

Override 4 · The payout container becomes a choice rather than an accident

Today: money lands wherever the chosen channel happens to deliver, and moving it costs another fee and another trip.

With RemitOS: the destination is part of the optimisation. Binimoy already provides the rails to move between banks, MFS and PSPs;[17][19] what has been missing is anything choosing on the recipient's behalf.

10.4 What might be wrong with our thesis

Three ways the central argument could fail, stated as they would be by a sceptical judge:

1. The incumbent simply builds it. bKash could add routing intelligence. The counter-argument is structural rather than optimistic: a rail has no incentive to build a product that ranks it against competitors, and neutrality cannot be retrofitted onto a party with a stake in the answer. That said, bKash could partner with an independent layer — somebody else's independent layer — and this remains the largest single commercial risk in the project. It carries a named owner in the risk register.

2. Partners refuse to cooperate. MFS operators have limited incentive to help a layer that commoditises them. The counter-arguments — incremental inbound volume they do not currently capture, and formalising flow that presently goes to hundi — are real but not guaranteed to persuade. If no payout partner agrees, the product does not exist.

3. The routing gains are smaller than assumed. If, in practice, corridor prices are tightly clustered, the value of choosing between them shrinks. We do not yet know, because we have not measured it — and the corridor cost scraper (n8n workflow 04) exists specifically to find out during Phase 3 validation rather than to assume.

12Technical architecture

A production architecture for a system that touches identity documents, money and regulation. Full detail lives in the repository; this chapter is the argument for why it is shaped this way.

12.1 The one-paragraph version

A send request enters through the Flutter client, is authenticated and risk-scored, and is handed to the routing engine, which solves a constrained optimisation over every available payout path. Compliance runs as a RAG-grounded process that cites the regulation behind each verdict or refuses to decide. The winning route is executed by a saga orchestrator against partner rails, written to a double-entry ledger, and reconciled nightly. Every step emits an immutable audit event.

12.3 Why bounded contexts rather than layers

The common failure is a "microservice" architecture that is really a distributed monolith: an api-service, a db-service, a logic-service, all sharing one database. Those are layers. They deploy together, fail together and scale together — all the cost of distribution with none of the benefit.

Our load profile is genuinely uneven, and that is the actual justification. The routing engine is CPU-bound and bursty. The ledger is write-heavy and must never lose data. The RAG service is embedding-bound. These cannot share a scaling policy.

So: one service per bounded context, each owning its own datastore. Services communicate by published event or by explicit API contract. No service reads another service's database.

13.3 Data science practice

Temporal splits, never random

A random split on transaction data leaks the future into the training set and produces a model that evaluates beautifully and fails in production. Every split in this project is by time. This is stated because it is the single most common methodological error in applied fintech ML, and it is invisible in the reported metrics — which is precisely what makes it dangerous.

Failures are retained in training

The corridor cost model trains on attempted legs including failures. A model trained only on successes systematically underestimates both cost and time, because retries are exactly where both accumulate.

Calibration, not just accuracy

A risk score that orders cases correctly but is badly calibrated misleads the officer reading it. Expected calibration error is a gate on every risk-bearing model.

Feature store and lineage

Features are defined once and served identically to training and inference, eliminating training-serving skew. Every feature carries lineage back to its source, so a prediction can be reconstructed years later — which is a regulatory requirement, not an engineering nicety.

14RAG and the knowledge layer

Retrieval-augmented generation applied to a domain where a confident wrong answer is a regulatory breach.

14.1 What RAG is, for the newcomer

A language model asked "may this transfer proceed under Bangladesh Bank rules?" will always produce a fluent answer, drawn from whatever it absorbed during training. It may be right. It cannot tell you which rule it relied on, and it does not know that the circular changed last Tuesday.

Retrieval-augmented generation fixes the mechanism rather than the prompt: before answering, the system searches an actual corpus of documents, retrieves the relevant passages, and requires the model to answer from those passages, citing them.

The difference is the difference between an opinion and a citation. In a regulated domain only one of the two is usable.

Sanctions data is deliberately not in the vector store Name screening is a deterministic matching problem — exact, phonetic, transliteration-aware, fuzzy, with tuned thresholds. Semantic similarity is the wrong tool: it would produce both misses and hits nobody can explain to a supervisor.

This matters especially in Bangla. রহিমা legitimately romanises as Rahima, Rohima or Rahema. A system doing exact string matching would miss real hits and generate unfair false positives against ordinary people with common names. Fuzzy plus phonetic matching is a fairness requirement as much as a compliance one.

14.3 Ingestion — where correctness is actually decided

Structure-aware chunking. Regulatory documents are hierarchical. A chunk that crosses a clause boundary produces an answer that cites the wrong rule. Chunks respect section boundaries, never split a table or a numbered clause, and retain their full heading path — so a citation reads "BRPD Circular 14/2025 § 3.2(b)" rather than a page number.

Effective-date metadata. Every chunk carries effective_from, effective_to, supersedes and superseded_by. Filtering on effective date happens before retrieval, not after.

This is not a refinement. Asking "what was the rule on 3 March" must not return today's rule, and a superseded circular is not merely irrelevant — applying it is wrong, and citing it makes the error look authoritative. Stale-rule leakage has a target of zero and is a hard release gate.

Language. Many circulars are published in Bangla only. The embedding model must be multilingual, and it is — which is also why it is self-hosted in-country rather than called as an API.

14.5 GraphRAG — the relational questions

Vector retrieval answers "what does the text say about X?" It cannot answer "is this beneficiary two hops from a flagged entity?" — and that second question is the one that catches laundering.

The hundi problem is structurally a network problem. Reporting on the CID investigation describes at least 5,000 MFS agents implicated in routing flows outside formal channels.[21] Those agents are not individually anomalous. They are anomalous as a pattern of shared beneficiaries, shared devices and circular funding — which a per-transaction classifier is blind to by construction.

TypologyGraph signature
SmurfingOne beneficiary receiving from many unrelated senders, each below threshold
Account farmingMany nominally unrelated accounts sharing one device fingerprint
LayeringCircular flow returning near its origin within a window
Hundi agent activityAn agent point with anomalous beneficiary concentration and off-pattern timing
Sanctions proximityShort path to a sanctioned entity node

Graph writes are fed from the event stream and are eventually consistent — deliberately off the synchronous authorisation path, because network risk is a review-time signal rather than a sub-second one. The retrieved subgraph is rendered to an officer as a diagram, which is considerably more persuasive in a case file than a score.

14.6 Guardrails

  1. Retrieved content is data, never instruction. Passages are delimited and the system prompt states that content inside the delimiter cannot issue instructions. Prompt injection via an ingested PDF is a live threat — an attacker who can influence a document in the corpus would otherwise be issuing commands to the compliance system.
  2. Cite or refuse. No passage above threshold means NOT_FOUND.
  3. Claim verification. Every factual assertion maps to a retrieved span, or it is stripped and the answer marked partial.
  4. Refusal is a first-class outcome — routed to a human, measured as a KPI, and never optimised downward. Driving the refusal rate down is how a safety mechanism gets quietly destroyed.
  5. Tokenisation before any external model call (Chapter 16).
  6. Confidence expressed in words, not numbers, on user surfaces.

15.6 Two workflows that carry unusual weight

Workflow 02 — sanctions list sync. It does not merely fetch a list. It re-screens the entire existing customer book against the daily diff. A name added to a sanctions list today may match a customer onboarded two years ago, and a system that screens only at transaction time will not notice until they transact — which may be never.

Workflow 07 — regulatory circular watcher. This is the mechanism that keeps the ceiling constraints current, and therefore the mechanism that makes the central design decision of this system safe. When Bangladesh Bank changes an MFS ceiling, the workflow detects it, re-indexes the corpus, and flags the change for human confirmation before the new value reaches configuration.

Extraction is automated; application is gated. An LLM reading "Tk 30,000" out of a legal document, in two languages, where a misplaced digit shifts a regulatory ceiling by an order of magnitude, is exactly the case where a human confirmation costing minutes prevents a system-wide misconfiguration.

The full twelve-workflow catalogue is in Appendix C.

15.7 Failing loud

Every workflow carries a dead-man's switch: the absence of a success heartbeat pages on-call. A silently failing sanctions sync is a compliance breach that accrues daily, and a workflow that has stopped running generates no error to notice. Its silence must therefore be the alarm.

16.2 How the boundary is enforced

Policy without a mechanism is a wish. Four mechanisms, in order of reliability.

Tokenisation proxy

inside   "Rahima Begum, NID 1990123456789, BDT 45,000 from Riyadh, 3rd transfer this week"
             ↓  tokenise at the boundary
outside  "<PERSON_A>, <ID_1>, <AMOUNT_1> from <CORRIDOR_SA>, 3rd transfer this week"
             ↓  external model reasons about structure and typology
inside       ↓  re-hydrate

The external model reasons about pattern, which is what it is genuinely good at. It never sees identity, which it does not need in order to observe that a frequency and amount pattern resembles structuring.

Egress filter

A filter in front of every external call pattern-matches NID formats, Bangladeshi phone numbers, account-number shapes and known name lists. A match fails the request and raises a security event. It does not warn and proceed.

CI enforcement

The same detector runs in CI against every prompt template and test fixture. A runtime filter catches a leak at the moment it would happen, in production, on a real user's data. CI catches it before the template is merged. Both are needed; only one is preventive.

In-boundary model hosting

Embeddings, reranking, Bangla ASR/TTS and all tabular ML run in-country. Voice is biometric data, and shipping it abroad for convenience would be a sovereignty breach dressed as an integration choice.

16.3 What this costs us

Stating the cost is part of stating the position honestly: in-boundary GPU hosting is more expensive than API calls; the tokenisation proxy adds latency and complexity; tokenised context measurably degrades some reasoning quality — which must be measured, not assumed acceptable; some managed services cannot be used at all; and disaster recovery requires geographically separated in-country capacity rather than a cheaper region abroad.

All accepted. A sovereignty claim that costs nothing is usually a sovereignty claim that is not being kept.

16.4 The Travel Rule exception, stated openly

Cross-border payments are inherently cross-border in data as well as money: a partner in Riyadh must receive beneficiary details in order to settle. We therefore distinguish two cases. Transaction data required for settlement is shared with the specific partner, under the FATF Travel Rule, minimised to what the rule requires, and logged per disclosure with its legal basis. Everything else is never shared.

This is an obligation, not a residency breach — and the distinction only holds because every disclosure is recorded.

17Responsible AI

Congress §15 makes responsible AI a first-class judging dimension. This chapter states what we actually do about it, mechanically.

17.1 The asymmetry that governs everything here

A false positive on an AML model is not a statistic. It is a held transfer. The money was often for medicine, school fees or rent. The person affected has no relationship manager to call, frequently no way to find out why, and — because the hold arrives at the worst possible moment — every incentive to conclude that the formal system does not work for people like her.

Stated plainly A 2 % false-positive rate concentrated on rural, older, low-device-tier recipients is not an acceptable model. It is discrimination with a confusion matrix.

This is why group-wise false-positive rate is a release gate in this project and not a line in a quarterly report.

17.2 Fairness, as an enforced gate

Every risk-bearing model is evaluated across: gender · age band · division (rural vs urban) · corridor of origin · transfer-size band · account tenure · device tier.

GateThreshold
False-positive rate ratio, worst group vs best≤ 1.25
Equal opportunity difference (TPR gap)≤ 0.05
Demographic parity difference≤ 0.05

Why device tier is called out specifically

Device tier is a wealth proxy. A model that scores older, cheaper Android handsets as higher-risk has learned to penalise poverty — and it will do so while reporting excellent aggregate accuracy, because poorer users are a minority of transaction volume.

This is the single most likely way this system becomes unjust, it is invisible to any single-number gate, and it is therefore checked explicitly on every model. A model that is 96 % accurate overall and 78 % accurate for rural women over 50 has failed, and it would pass any aggregate threshold you care to set.

17.3 Label bias, and its honest limit

The AML model trains on officer adjudications, which carry their own historical bias. A model can only reproduce and amplify that; it cannot correct it. This is stated as a known limitation in the model card rather than mitigated rhetorically.

The partial mitigation is periodic blind re-adjudication of a random sample — including cases the model passed — which surfaces systematic divergence between what officers decide with the model and what they decide without it.

17.4 Human authority

Mandatory human gates: any AML score above threshold · any sanctions hit, without exception, regardless of confidence · any transfer above the corridor review threshold · any override of a prior BLOCK · any change to a regulatory limit or fee schedule · any model promotion to production.

Each gate records who decided, when, what they saw — the exact evidence bundle presented to them — and why. The "what they saw" element is snapshotted rather than re-derived later, because a decision can only be judged against the information the decider actually had.

17.6 Responsible AI in the user interface

  • FX guidance is suppressed on urgent transfers. A model that delays medicine money to capture thirty basis points has misunderstood what it is for.

The lever that most improves scalability is therefore not infrastructure — it is reducing the false-positive rate, because every avoided unnecessary escalation is simultaneously a user not harmed and a reviewer-hour freed. Fairness and scalability point in the same direction here, which is unusual and worth exploiting.

19.3 Cost curve

Costs fall into three shapes, and knowing which is which is what makes the model predictable:

  • Fixed — the platform, the corpus, the evaluation suite. Amortised across every transfer, so the curve improves with volume.
  • Linear — partner rail costs, payout costs, human review. These do not improve with scale, which is why they must be modelled per transfer rather than assumed away.
  • Step — in-boundary GPU capacity, review team headcount, a new corridor integration. Each step is a discrete decision with a lead time attached.

20Go-to-market and the regulatory path

The chapter most student fintech projects skip, and the one that determines whether the product can legally exist.

20.1 What we are, legally

RemitOS as designed does not hold customer funds, operate as a money transfer operator, issue accounts or wallets, or set exchange rates. It orchestrates across licensed parties.

That is a deliberate regulatory posture, not a limitation discovered late: the orchestration layer is both the defensible product and the only position a new entrant can occupy without years of licensing first.

We are, however, not licence-free. Processing payment instructions and personal data on behalf of licensed institutions carries its own obligations, which is why the compliance, audit and ledger services are v1 scope rather than v2 ambitions.

25Domain-pack extensibility

Why this architecture outlives one idea — and how any FinSphere vertical drops into the same core.

25.1 The split

Roughly 70 % of what any FinSphere product needs is identical: identity, KYC, a ledger, risk scoring, a knowledge layer, agents, automation, observability, audit. The core knows nothing about remittance. Each vertical is a swappable domain pack supplying exactly four things and nothing else:

ElementContents
ConfigFeature flags, limits, rails, fee schedules, workflow bindings
SchemasDomain entities and events, versioned
PromptsSystem prompts, tool descriptions, retrieval templates
ModelsDomain-specific ML models and their cards

The core exposes stable extension points and never imports from a pack. The governing rule: a core change made to accommodate a vertical requires a new ADR — and if that happens twice for the same reason, the abstraction is wrong and gets redrawn.

25.2 The five FinSphere packs

PackChallenge areaWhat it reuses unchangedStatus
borderless_moneyCross-border payments and remittance intelligence—Active
creditCreditForAll — alternative-data credit scoringIdentity, KYC, ledger, explainability, fairness gates, audit. Plus: a verified remittance history is exactly the alternative data a thin-file borrower lacksStub
fraudFraudShield — real-time transaction defenceGraph, scoring pipeline, case management, human gates — already built here as a componentStub
wealthWealthPilot — advisory for everyday saversRAG with citation enforcement (suitability rules), personalisation, explainabilityStub
smeSME Capital Engine — working-capital intelligenceLedger, forecasting, document intelligence, compliance decisioningStub

The credit row is the strategically interesting one. A two-year verified remittance history is a genuine credit signal for a household with no formal financial record — which means the adjacent vertical is not a pivot but a compounding use of the same data, created as a by-product of the first product working.

25.3 Beyond FinSphere

Nothing in the core is finance-specific. The pattern — a domain-agnostic platform with identity, a knowledge layer, bounded agents, human gates, automation and audit — transfers to any regulated domain the Congress names. A HealthSphere pack would swap the corpus for clinical guidelines and the models for triage; the human gates, the residency boundary, the citation enforcement and the fairness machinery are identical because the underlying requirement is identical: an AI system making consequential decisions about people who need to be able to challenge them.

Why this was built this way A remittance orchestrator that cannot extend into credit or fraud is a feature, not a company. Building the abstraction from a real product — rather than speculatively ahead of one — is what makes it likely to be right. The four stub packs declare their extension points; they do not implement features nobody has validated.

0:00 – 0:30 · Problem — clarity, relevance

A construction worker in Riyadh sends forty-five thousand taka home to his mother every month. He doesn't know which of three providers will actually deliver the most money. Neither does anyone else — because nobody computes it.

Bangladesh received thirty-five and a half billion dollars in remittances last fiscal year. Seventh in the world. The World Bank puts the global cost of sending money at six point three six percent, against a UN target of three.

And here is the number that matters: roughly half the money coming into Bangladesh doesn't come through the formal system at all. It comes through hundi — because hundi is faster, the rate is better, and nobody asks for paperwork.

0:30 – 1:00 · Solution — simplicity, uniqueness

We are not building another wallet. bKash, Nagad and the banks are rails — and rails are not the gap.

The gap is the decision. Which rail, which exchange window, which payout point delivers the most taka, soonest, legally. Today that decision is a guess.

RemitOS is an intelligence layer that makes it. Machine-learned routing across every rail, compliance automation grounded in the actual regulation with citations, and predictive send-timing — in Bangla, and reachable on a feature phone with no internet.

1:00 – 2:00 · Demo — feasibility, logic

He enters forty-five thousand taka. We show three routes — and we lead with what reaches her, not with the fee. Two hours longer, three hundred and twenty taka more. In Bangla, in one line.

Now watch the case nobody designs for. He sends one hundred and twenty thousand — a medical emergency. Bangladesh Bank caps agent cash-out at thirty thousand a day.

Every other product quotes it, takes his money, and his mother finds out at the counter — after a forty-minute journey, during an emergency.

We know the limit before we quote. So we propose a legal split: thirty thousand to the wallet today, ninety to her bank account tomorrow. Both priced. Both explained. And she hears it as a voice call in Bangla, because she does not have a smartphone.

2:00 – 2:30 · AI approach — depth, structure

Four AI layers, and none of them is a chatbot.

Machine learning predicts landed cost and arrival time per rail. Retrieval over Bangladesh Bank circulars and FATF guidance answers compliance questions — and if it cannot find the rule, it refuses and calls a human. It never guesses at regulation. A knowledge graph catches what per-transaction scoring cannot: the network signature of hundi agent activity. And agents investigate alerts and build the evidence file — but no agent moves money. Every irreversible action has a human gate.

Personal data never leaves Bangladesh. We tokenise before any external model call, and an egress filter blocks it if we get it wrong.

Every one percent of cost removed from a thirty-five billion dollar flow is three hundred and fifty million dollars a year staying with Bangladeshi families.

We measure landed amount per hundred dollars sent, time to the recipient's hand, and the false-positive rate broken down by group — because a held transfer is somebody's medicine, and it should not be held more often because she lives in a village.
